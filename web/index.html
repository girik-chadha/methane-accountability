Why methane matters

Every figure in this tool traces to a public source. Here is what the numbers mean and why a gas leak is an environmental emergency rather than an accounting problem.

The gas that is leaking

Natural gas is mostly methane. When a well, pipeline or processing plant vents or leaks, methane goes straight into the atmosphere unburned — and methane traps far more heat than the same mass of carbon dioxide.

Over 20 years, a tonne of fossil methane warms the planet about 82.5 times as much as a tonne of CO₂. Over 100 years, about 29.8 times. Both figures come from the same IPCC table. The difference matters: methane breaks down in roughly a decade, so its damage is concentrated in the near term — which is exactly the window in which we are trying to avoid crossing 1.5°C.

That is why this tool lets you switch between the 20-year and 100-year view. Neither is wrong. The 20-year figure describes the heat you get this decade; the 100-year figure is the one used in most official reporting.

How much warming methane has already caused

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report attributes 0.5°C of the 1.07°C of observed warming (2010–2019 against 1850–1900) to methane emissions, with a range of 0.3 to 0.8°C. That makes methane the second largest driver of warming after carbon dioxide, causing roughly two thirds as much warming as CO₂ has.

A note on a number you will see elsewhere. Many articles say methane causes "about a third of global warming." Scientists writing in Frontiers in Science specifically ask people to stop using that phrasing, because it blurs emissions with concentrations and invites misreading. This tool quotes the IPCC's 0.5°C instead. We would rather be checkable than punchy.

What it does to people, not just the climate

Methane is a precursor to ground-level ozone, the main ingredient of smog. Ozone damages human lungs and plant tissue, so methane harms people and food supply directly, on top of the warming.

UNEP and the Climate and Clean Air Coalition assessed what cutting human-caused methane 45% this decade would achieve. Annually, it would prevent:

260,000premature deaths 775,000asthma-related hospital visits 73bnhours of labour lost to extreme heat 25 Mtof crop losses

It would also avoid nearly 0.3°C of warming by 2045, which is why the same assessment calls methane the strongest lever available for slowing warming in the next 25 years.

We do not divide these figures per leak. Ozone forms regionally from many sources, so no share of those deaths can be honestly assigned to one pipeline. These are global figures describing what methane does. The per-leak numbers in this tool are only the things that can be measured for that leak: how much gas, how much warming, how much money.

What the units mean

kg/h — kilograms per hour
The leak rate. How much methane escapes every hour. A typical leak here runs at about 2,900 kg/h, which is roughly three tonnes of gas an hour, continuously.
tonne (t)
1,000 kilograms — about the weight of a small car.
kilotonne (kt)
1,000 tonnes, or a million kilograms. So "99.6 kt of methane per day" means 99,600 tonnes of gas entering the atmosphere daily.
CO₂e — carbon dioxide equivalent
Methane converted into the amount of CO₂ that would cause the same warming, so different gases can be compared on one scale. Multiply methane by 29.8 for the 100-year view or 82.5 for the 20-year view.
GWP — global warming potential
Those multipliers. The number after it is the time horizon: GWP-20 or GWP-100.
Cars' yearly emissions
We convert CO₂e using the US EPA's official equivalency figure of 4.29 tonnes of CO₂e per average passenger vehicle per year. So "485 cars" means one day of that leak causes the warming that 485 cars cause in a whole year of driving.
Homes supplied
The wasted gas expressed as household gas use, using the EPA's figure for an average home's annual gas consumption. This gas was a saleable product; leaking it wastes the energy and causes the warming at the same time.

Why these leaks are the fixable kind

Unlike most climate problems, large methane leaks are concentrated, visible from orbit and often profitable to stop — the gas escaping is the product the operator sells. The UN already finds these leaks by satellite and notifies governments free of charge through its Methane Alert and Response System.

The failure is administrative, not technical. In 2024, of roughly 1,200 notifications sent, about 15 got a reply. Where a country has appointed a single named official to receive alerts, responses run near a third of the time rather than around one in eight. Plugging leaks in Algeria and Nigeria removed warming equivalent to taking about a million cars off the road.

That is what this tool is for: showing exactly which alerts went unanswered, what each one is doing to the atmosphere, and who could plausibly close it.
